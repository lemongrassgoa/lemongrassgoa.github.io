A Short History of Classical Music

A simple journey through some composers and compositions from the Medieval, Renaissance, Baroque, Classical, Romantic and twentieth-century periods.

Classical music developed over many centuries. Musical styles changed gradually, with composers borrowing ideas from earlier generations while introducing new forms, instruments and ways of expressing emotion.

The composers below are arranged broadly in chronological order. Each entry gives a little background about the composer, their musical period and the particular work or movement in the list.

Medieval Music

Medieval music covers roughly the period from the 5th to the early 15th century. Much of the earliest surviving music is sacred and consists of single melodic lines, particularly Gregorian chant.

Later in the period, composers began developing polyphony, in which several independent musical lines sound together. This development laid important foundations for the Renaissance.

Some Medieval composers

Hildegard of Bingen, Léonin, Pérotin, Guillaume de Machaut, Philippe de Vitry and Adam de la Halle.

Fantasia No. 1

Robert White (1538–1574) — Renaissance

The composer

Robert White was an English Renaissance composer and church musician. His music belongs to the rich English tradition of contrapuntal writing that developed during the sixteenth century.

The composition

A fantasia is a free instrumental form in which the composer can develop musical ideas without following the strict structure of a dance. White's music uses imitation and interweaving melodic lines to create intricate Renaissance counterpoint.

Quadro Pavane / Galliard

Richard Allison (c.1570–c.1600) — Renaissance

The composer

Richard Allison was an English composer and viol player of the late Renaissance. He wrote instrumental music at a time when music-making at home was becoming increasingly popular.

The composition

The pavane was a slow and stately Renaissance dance, while the galliard was quicker and more energetic. The pairing creates a contrast between dignity and lively movement.

Other Renaissance composers:

Thomas Tallis, William Byrd, John Dowland, Maddalena Casulana, Caterina Assandra

Canon and Gigue in D, P.37

Johann Pachelbel (1653–1706) — Baroque

Featured movement: 2. Gigue

The composer

Johann Pachelbel was a German Baroque composer and organist. He was an important figure in the musical life of southern Germany and was part of the generation that preceded J. S. Bach.

The composition

The Canon is famous for its repeating harmonic pattern. The Gigue that follows is much livelier, using the characteristic rhythms of a fast Baroque dance.

The Four Seasons: Winter

Antonio Vivaldi (1678–1741) — Baroque

Featured movement: 2. Largo

The composer

Antonio Vivaldi was an Italian composer and virtuoso violinist. He was one of the most important composers of the Baroque concerto and helped establish the contrast between soloist and orchestra.

The composition

Winter is one of Vivaldi's four violin concertos known collectively as The Four Seasons. The Largo provides a peaceful contrast to the harsher winter scenes elsewhere in the concerto, creating an image of warmth and quiet indoors.

Cantata BWV 147: “Herz und Mund und Tat und Leben”

Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750) — Baroque

Featured movement: 10. “Jesu, bleibet meine Freude”

The composer

Johann Sebastian Bach is one of the most celebrated composers of the Baroque era. He was particularly important as a composer of sacred music, keyboard music and works based on intricate counterpoint.

The composition

BWV 147 is a sacred cantata. Its final chorale movement is one of Bach's most familiar melodies, with a calm vocal line set against a flowing accompaniment.

Water Music Suite No. 2, HWV 349

George Frideric Handel (1685–1759) — Baroque

Featured movement: 2. Alla hornpipe

The composer

George Frideric Handel was born in Germany but spent much of his career in England. He became famous for opera, oratorio and large-scale ceremonial music.

The composition

Handel's Water Music was written for a royal event on the River Thames. The famous hornpipe is bright, confident and highly rhythmic, making it particularly suited to a public celebration.

Water Music Suite No. 3, HWV 350

George Frideric Handel (1685–1759) — Baroque

Featured movement: 2. Rigaudon

The composer

Handel was one of the central figures of the late Baroque. Although born in Germany, he established his career in London and became one of the city's leading composers.

The composition

A rigaudon is a lively French dance. Handel turns its energetic rhythms into colourful orchestral music, giving this movement a bright and festive character.

Other Baroque composers

Arcangelo Corelli, Georg Philipp Telemann, Domenico Scarlatti, Barbara Strozzi, Élisabeth Jacquet de La Guerre

Eine Kleine Nachtmusik

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (1756–1791) — Classical

Featured movement: 3. Menuett

The composer

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was an Austrian composer whose music represents many of the ideals of the Classical period: balance, clarity, elegance and carefully controlled musical form.

The composition

Eine Kleine Nachtmusik is one of Mozart's best-known works for strings. Its third movement is a graceful minuet, combining a formal dance rhythm with Mozart's characteristic melodic elegance.

6 Minuets

Ludwig van Beethoven (1770–1827) — Classical / Early Romantic

Featured movement: 2. Minuet in G

The composer

Ludwig van Beethoven stands between the Classical and Romantic eras. His early music follows the traditions of Mozart and Haydn, while his later works expanded the emotional and structural possibilities of classical music.

The composition

The second minuet has the elegant and measured character expected of a Classical dance. Its simplicity makes a useful contrast with the dramatic music Beethoven would later become famous for.

Piano Sonata No. 8, Op. 13

Ludwig van Beethoven (1770–1827) — Classical / Early Romantic

Featured movement: 2. Adagio cantabile

The composer

Beethoven helped carry the Classical style into the Romantic era. His music often combines formal structure with strong emotional contrasts and dramatic expression.

The composition

Known as the Pathétique Sonata, this is one of Beethoven's most famous early piano works. The Adagio cantabile is its lyrical centre, with a slow and expressive melody that contrasts with the dramatic outer movements.

Other Classical / early Romantic composers:

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach, Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, Joseph Haydn, Luigi Boccherini, Marianna Martines, Maria Theresia von Paradis

String Quartet in E-flat

Fanny Hensel (née Mendelssohn) (1805–1847) — Romantic

Featured movement: 1. Adagio ma non troppo

The composer

Fanny Hensel was a highly accomplished German composer and pianist, and the sister of Felix Mendelssohn. She wrote music in many genres and demonstrated considerable skill in harmony, counterpoint and chamber music.

The composition

Hensel's string quartet shows the expressive intensity of nineteenth-century chamber music. The opening Adagio ma non troppo creates a reflective atmosphere while allowing the four instruments to interact closely.

Gran Vals

Francisco Tárrega (1852–1909) — Romantic

The composer

Francisco Tárrega was a Spanish guitarist and composer who played an important role in establishing the modern classical guitar repertoire. His music combines Romantic expression with the particular colours of the guitar.

The composition

Gran Vals is a lively guitar waltz. A short section later became famous around the world because it was used as the recognizable Nokia ringtone.

Other Romantic composers:

Felix Mendelssohn, Robert Schumann, Clara Schumann, Franz Schubert, Johannes Brahms, Isaac Albéniz, Enrique Granados, Camille Saint-Saëns, Antonín Dvořák, Edvard Grieg

Suite for Variety Orchestra

Dmitri Shostakovich (1906–1975) — 20th Century

Featured movement: 7. Waltz II

The composer

Dmitri Shostakovich was one of the major composers of the twentieth century. Working under the Soviet regime, he developed a musical language that could combine lyricism, humour, irony and darker dramatic qualities.

The composition

Waltz II is among Shostakovich's most recognizable pieces. Its memorable waltz rhythm and distinctive orchestral colours give it an unusual mixture of elegance, humour and uneasiness.

Other composers from this period

Igor Stravinsky, Sergei Prokofiev, Sergei Rachmaninoff, Maurice Ravel, Béla Bartók.
